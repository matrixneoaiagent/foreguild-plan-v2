Foreguild should not be a job board that takes a fee. It should be a completion underwriter: it quotes a fixed price and date for a verified outcome, carries the loss if the work fails, and keeps the spread between the price of certainty and the cost of delivery. The board is a distribution channel. The guarantee is the product.

Agent labour is abundant. What is scarce is a buyer willing to pay for an outcome that is certain enough to plan around. Every plan on this bounty says some version of that. Almost none prices it. Verification is described as the moat, then booked as a cost line. The obvious question is never asked: if verification is the product, what does it cost when the product fails?

That question turns a marketplace P&L into an underwriting P&L. A 10% take on a $150 task is $15; a 10% human audit is $19. ASSUMPTION The fee cannot pay for the check. A 10% sample of a $19 audit is $1.90 per task; at a 6% fee, the fee covers the sample only above $31.67, and gas pushes the floor to about $37.50. The field's own numbers reach for risk transfer; this plan names it as the business.

Name it. A $6,000 remediation contract can carry a $360 guarantee premium (6%) against $168 of expected claims (2.8%), $36 of verification (0.6%) and $6 of payment (0.1%) — a 41.7% contribution margin on the premium. The underlying contract, if Foreguild is principal, carries a 36% contribution margin after the worker, rework, verification and payment, before fixed costs and CAC. The same economics do not exist on a $15 task. The guarantee is why the ticket can be large; the ticket is why the guarantee can be cheap.

Why futarchy. The fee is a lever; the loss ratio is the survival variable. The consensus that emerges is coherent: a 6–12% take on escrowed work, worker bonds, a dispute ladder, and futarchy over a 90-day net-revenue metric. It is also a fee business, and a fee business has a ceiling. The fee has to cover verification, payment, support and the cost of failure. At a $150 average ticket, a 10% fee is $15. A single 20-minute human audit at $57/hour is $19. ASSUMPTION The arithmetic forces one of three outcomes: no human audit, a much larger ticket, or a different product.

The field knows this. The arithmetic is simple: a 10% sample of a $19 audit is $1.90 per task; at a 6% fee, the fee covers the sample only above $31.67, and gas pushes the floor to about $37.50. An 18% acceptance-guarantee tier with a priced reserve reaches for the same thing, but it is still attached to a take-rate business. The guarantee is an upsell. The core revenue is still a commission on labour, which is the most competitive, least defensible part of the stack.

The category error. A 2% assurance add-on on a $150 task is $3. Expected claims at a 2.8% loss ratio are $4.20. The add-on is underpriced before any verification cost. Insurance is not a feature you bolt onto a commission; it is a separate business with its own capital, pricing, reserves and regulator. If Foreguild wants the margin, it has to run the business, not the upsell.

The alternative is to make the guarantee the product and the board the distribution. Then the questions change. What is the expected loss by task class? What does a verification failure cost? How much reserve is enough? Which risks should be declined? Those are the questions a futarchy is actually good at answering, because they have measurable, delayed outcomes and a real cost to being wrong. They are also the questions that determine whether the company makes money.

The evidence that this is the gap, not a niche of one: the two closest live markets both have a verification bottleneck and a funded buyer. Cantina has paid $56.8M to researchers and lists 52 live opportunities; the volume is not the constraint: 8,450 vulnerabilities have been found, and closing and verifying them is the remaining job.8 Poidh shows the same pattern: 73 claims on this bounty, and a long tail of bounties with more claims than awards; judging, not supply, is the visible bottleneck.28 Its escrow-and-claim model, documented publicly, is the closest live analogue to the board Foreguild wants to build.29 In both cases, the scarce good is a credible completion, not another worker.

What is genuinely new here

  • A loss-ratio market, not a fee market. Seer prices the pure premium; the price is used in every quote.
  • A completion guarantee as the unit of sale. The buyer buys certainty, not agent labour.
  • RAC90 with a reserve ratio guardrail. Revenue cannot be bought by underpricing risk.
  • A verification P&L. Cost per check, error targets, appeals and calibration are in the model.
  • A measured governance constraint. 1,154 conditional Seer markets, 127 funded, median funded liquidity $25.92; the plan is designed around that, not around a fantasy of deep markets.

What this plan does not claim

  • No live Foreguild revenue, users or partners. Foreguild is proposed.
  • No token, no token sale, no liquidity mining.
  • No claim to be an insurer. The product is a contractual performance guarantee, sold to businesses, initially from the company's own balance sheet.
  • No claim that Seer markets are deep enough today to bind billion-dollar decisions. The binding cap is $50k until depth is proven.
  • No claim that the 46.7% loss ratio is observed. It is a target; the first 100 claims test it.

Beachhead 1: the finding is the specification

An audit finding is an unusually good task. It has a written description, a severity, a code location, and a natural acceptance test: reproduce the issue on the vulnerable commit, write a test that fails, implement the fix, show the test passes, and show the rest of the suite still passes. The deliverable is not a PDF; it is a diff plus an evidence packet. The buyer is not choosing between 40 agents; they need one bonded team to close the issue before the next deployment.

The economics are equally unusual. A high-severity finding can block a launch or a TVL increase. The alternative to a $6,000 fixed-price fix is a delayed release or a re-audit, both of which cost more. The buyer's willingness to pay is anchored to the cost of the delay, not to the hourly rate of a developer. That is the definition of a good guarantee market: the buyer's loss is large, the worker's cost is small, and the acceptance test is cheap relative to both.

Demand signal

Cantina: $58.9M of payouts available, $56.8M paid, 8,450 vulnerabilities found, 25,136 researchers.8 The scarce side is closing and verifying, not finding.

Supply signal

DefiLlama: 927 protocols on Base, 257 with >$1M TVL, 146 with >$10M; Base TVL $5.75B.9 A funded protocol is a buyer with a security budget.

Verification signal

The acceptance test is a command. The evidence is a hash. The dispute is a diff. This is the cheapest verification tier Foreguild will ever have — and the one that can support a guarantee on day one.

Beachhead 2: reconciliation is recurring, deterministic and boring

Every DAO and fund has a version of the same problem: the chain has the truth, the spreadsheet has a story, and the auditor wants both. A 12-month reconciliation is a set of deterministic checks — balances, transfers, cost basis, unrealized P&L, counterparty exposure — wrapped in a workpaper. Agents are good at this; humans are expensive and bored by it. The buyer pays every quarter, which is the closest thing to subscription revenue in a services business. The verification is objective: re-pull a 10% sample, assert balances on-chain, require a tx hash for every line. The guarantee is smaller (a missed transaction is a restatement, not an exploit), but the recurrence is higher.

Beachhead 3: sell the harness, not the labour

AI labs and agent platforms need evaluation sets that are private, hard and executable. A public benchmark is contaminated; a vendor's eval is a black box. Foreguild can sell a harness: 50–200 tasks, each with a setup, a prompt, an acceptance test and a gold label. The buyer runs it against their agent; the output is a score with a reproducible trace. The margin is high because the harness is reusable, and the work compounds Foreguild's own verification library. It is third, not first, because procurement is slower and the NDA burden is higher.

Why not compete with Upwork

The incumbent market is not growing. Upwork's FY2025 10-K reports $4.0B of GSV, flat with 2024 and down from $4.1B in 2023; revenue was $787.8M, a 19.7% take.6 Fiverr's FY2025 20-F reports $1.073B of marketplace GMV, down 2.2%, with annual active buyers down 13.6% and spend per buyer up 13.3%; its marketplace take rate was 27.7%.7 The low end is shrinking; the high end is where spend is growing. A new general board would compete on breadth against two public companies with a decade of liquidity. A risk layer can sit on top of both: it does not need to own the buyer relationship to underwrite the outcome.

03 / Product and agent swarmHow agents are assigned: one contract, ten states, twelve agents.

The product is a completion guarantee. Foreguild quotes a fixed price and a delivery date for a scoped outcome. The buyer funds escrow. Foreguild assigns the work to a bonded worker or its own swarm, verifies the acceptance test, and releases payment. If the work fails, the buyer gets a remedy: rework, a refund, or a payout from the guarantee reserve. Foreguild keeps the difference between the contract price and the cost of delivery, plus a premium if the buyer buys the guarantee separately. There are three surfaces:

Guaranteed Delivery

Foreguild is principal. Fixed price, fixed date, full delivery risk. The core revenue line in Years 1–2. Average contract $5,000–$6,000; 36% contribution margin before fixed costs.

Assured Board

Foreguild is broker and guarantor. Third parties post escrowed work with a T0; Foreguild routes it, takes 9%, and sells a 6% completion guarantee. The scalable line.

Verify API

Foreguild sells the verification harness to other marketplaces, DAOs and data teams: $1 per check, $500–$5,000/month retainers. 70% contribution margin.

The lifecycle. Every state writes a record: spec hash, worker bond, evidence bundle, verdict, payment, claim, loss. The record is the training data for the next quote and the audit trail for the next dispute.

Reputation and staking: make bad work expensive, not impossible

Foreguild does not need a global reputation token. It needs a record that travels and a bond that can be slashed. Every settled task writes a signed attestation: category, acceptance outcome, on-time flag, audit result, dispute outcome, verifier consensus. The score is a Wilson 95% lower bound on the pass rate per category, not a star rating. A worker with 18 of 20 passes has a lower bound around 74%; a worker with 2 of 2 passes has a lower bound around 34%. The bound punishes small samples and rewards consistency, which is exactly what a buyer needs.

Staking rules

  • Worker bond: 10% of task value, capped at $2,500. Returned 14 days after acceptance. Slashed on a proven fake, an abandoned task, or a failed audit that the worker refuses to cure.
  • Verifier bond: 20% of the verification fee, minimum $500. Slashed if an appeal overturns the verdict and the verifier was the deciding vote.
  • Buyer deposit: 20% at posting for new buyers above $10k; refundable. Protects workers from scope creep and non-payment.
  • Concentration: no worker above 20% of monthly guaranteed value; no class above 35% of the reserve; no buyer above 25% of revenue.

Why not a token

A transferable reputation token is a security, a sybil magnet and a distraction. Foreguild's reputation is non-transferable and earned by work. The only financial stake is the bond, which is posted in USDC and returned. This keeps the incentive aligned without creating a second market to govern.

If a token is ever used, it should be a non-transferable work credential, not a speculative asset. The plan does not assume one.

For a guarantee sold on a board task, the premium is priced from the expected claims cost, not from the fee:

premium rate = PP × (1 + risk load 25%) + expense load 1.2% + capital charge 0.6% + target load 1.0 pt

If the Seer market forecasts a pure premium (PP) of 2.8% of guaranteed value, the technical premium is 3.5% + 1.2% + 0.6% + 1.0% = 6.3%. The technical loss ratio is 44.4%. The model prices at 6.0%, a 0.3-point discount to the technical price; the plan's 46.7% loss ratio is measured at the model price. If the market forecasts 4.5%, the technical premium is 8.4% — and if the buyer will not pay it, the risk is declined. That is the market doing the underwriting.

Read 6.3% as the technical price, not the model's price. The model uses 6.0% to reflect what a new underwriter can charge in its first classes; the 0.3-point gap is uncollected margin, and it is why the plan's 46.7% loss ratio is higher than the 44.4% technical loss ratio. The gap is a deliberate launch discount, not a missing cost; the underwriting core in §05 shows the full bridge.

Every plan on this bounty says verification is the moat. Few cost it. That is the difference between a moat and a slogan. “Claim leakage” is the share of premium paid on claims that should have been caught by verification. It is the single best measure of verification quality: a verifier that passes everything has low cost and high leakage; a verifier that fails everything has high cost and high appeals. Appeals: priced, bounded, published

Either side can appeal within 14 days by posting a bond of 10% of the task value (minimum $25, maximum $500). One appeal per task. A second verifier with a different model mix re-scores the same evidence; if the verdict flips, the bond is returned and the escrow follows the new verdict. If it does not, the bond pays the second verifier. Above $10,000, the appeal goes to a bonded three-person panel; above $25,000, the claim is referred to a Kleros-based arbitration agreement.13 The protocol takes nothing from the appeal; the bond is a filter, not a revenue line. Every appeal is published with the evidence, the verdict and the reasoning, so the next quote is better.

How verification quality is measured and improved

The calibration set

Every quarter, Foreguild labels a random 200-task set with two independent human reviewers and a third adjudicator. It computes false accept, false reject, Cohen's κ, cost per verdict and time to verdict. κ below 0.7 means the rubric is ambiguous; the rubric is rewritten before the next quarter. The set is stratified by class and ticket size, not convenience-sampled.

The improvement loop

Every false accept becomes a new harness check. Every appeal becomes a rubric edit. Every claim becomes a loss-data row. The harness library is versioned; the cost per check falls 10–15% per doubling of reuse (learning curve). The target is a 30% reduction in verification cost per $1,000 of guaranteed value by the end of Year 2, without raising the loss ratio.

Why this is a moat and not a cost centre. A competitor can copy the model, the prompts and the tier structure. It cannot copy the labeled loss data: which task class, which worker, which buyer, which rubric produced which claim. That data is the input to the next quote, the reserve and the Seer market. Every settled task makes the next guarantee cheaper to price and easier to sell. The verification cost is the price of generating that asset; the guarantee premium is the revenue from owning it.
The honest failure mode. If the measured false-accept rate stays above 5% after 500 tasks, the guarantee is underpriced and the T2 sample must rise. If it rises above 20%, the unit economics break and the guarantee should be withdrawn from that class. The plan says this before the first claim, not after the first exploit.

The model is most sensitive to three variables: average contract value, loss ratio, and guarantee attach rate. 07 / Futarchy governanceHow spending and disputes are governed: the underwriting committee that is paid to be right.

Futarchy's original rule is simple: vote on values, but bet on beliefs.1534 MetaDAO has run 96 proposals for 14 organizations since November 2023, so the mechanism is no longer only a thought experiment.14 What is still unsettled is where it binds. That is a design choice, not a slogan.

Most futarchy plans on this bounty govern the fee. The fee matters, but it is not the survival variable. A one-point fee change on $6.8M of GMV is $68k; a 10-point loss-ratio miss on $444k of premium is $44k, a quarter of the guarantee contribution, and a 30-point miss leaves almost no margin before fixed costs. The loss ratio is exactly the kind of variable a conditional market can forecast: delayed, measurable, and expensive to get wrong.

RAC90 — Risk-Adjusted Contribution over a trailing 90 days.

RAC90 = net revenue (fees + premiums + API + float + data)
− claims paid − verification cost − payment cost
− change in incurred-but-not-reported reserve − capital charge (8% × required reserve)

Why not revenue: revenue can be bought by underpricing risk. Why not profit: profit can be bought by cutting verification. RAC includes both the cost of failure and the cost of checking. It is computed from the treasury, the claims ledger and the verification invoices, and it is published monthly.

The constitution: five rules no market can override

  1. Solvency. The claims reserve must be at least 1.5× the next 90 days of expected claims. If it falls below 120%, underwriting stops automatically until a market and the board restore it.
  2. Legality. No guarantee for illegal work, sanctioned parties, or consumer buyers. The legal wrapper's board has a fiduciary duty and can veto an illegal or insolvent decision.
  3. Custody. Escrow and reserve sit in a 2-of-3 Safe. No market can move funds directly; the treasurer executes only approved, line-item spends.
  4. Metric integrity. The RAC90 definition can only change through a 90-day delayed market plus board ratification. The Auditor publishes the SQL, the addresses and the claims ledger.
  5. Safety pause. A 2-of-3 steward committee can pause for 72 hours for illegality, sanctions, security or a broken oracle. It must publish a reason and submit a ratification market within 7 days. Silence ratifies the pause; a “resume” market can reverse it.

How a decision market is built on Seer

Seer's conditional market primitive is the right tool: a parent market resolves the decision, and child markets resolve the metric under each branch.1 We use a parent binary and two conditional scalar markets on RAC90. The parent is not a token-price futarchy market; Foreguild has no token and will not invent one. It is a decision market with a measured outcome.

The decision structure. The parent resolves whether the proposal is adopted; the two child markets price RAC90 under adoption and status quo. The RAC90 branches are scalar markets with a +$500k offset because Seer bounds are uint256: a realised −$500k resolves at 0, and +$2M at $2.5M. The branch that does not happen becomes obsolete: the child's collateral is the parent's wrapped outcome token, which is worthless if the parent resolves the other way. Foreguild seeds both branches and books the losing seed as a decision cost, not a refundable deposit. This is the standard conditional-market structure Seer documents.1

Seer's own thesis is that prediction markets will increasingly be consumed by algorithms at machine speed, not only by humans reading a headline.30 A loss-ratio market is exactly that: a recurring, resolvable number that an underwriting agent can read and act on. Base hosts the cheaper advisory markets and the customer-facing receipts.
  • Collateral: USDC or sDAI on Gnosis, whichever the market supports; the plan does not assume a Foreguild token.

Kleros is the claims-appeal backstop for claims above $25k, not part of Seer's market resolution.113
  • TWAP: seven days of trading, 72-hour TWAP before the decision, 48-hour timelock before execution. The TWAP is computed from Seer's public trade data and published with the report. No single-block prints, no last-minute candles.
  • Fail branch: the status-quo branch is seeded with the same budget as the pass branch. The seed is at risk and is budgeted as a decision cost, not a refundable deposit. If it is too thin to price, the decision is advisory, not binding. This directly addresses the “fail-conditional market goes quiet” failure that Frontier Research documents in MetaDAO's implementation.17

The thin-market rule, in one formula. Let L be two-sided liquidity, N unique traders with non-zero volume, and T days of trading. The market weight is w = min(1, L/$25,000) × min(1, N/40) × min(1, T/14). If w < 0.30, the market is advisory and the actuary uses its own loss experience (w = 0). If 0.30 ≤ w < 0.70, the pure premium is w × PP_market + (1−w) × PP_actuary. If w ≥ 0.70 and L ≥ $5,000 and N ≥ 20, the market can bind up to $50,000; above that it needs L ≥ $25,000, N ≥ 40 and board ratification. Worked: L = $6,000, N = 22, T = 7 gives w = 0.066, so a 3.2% market forecast against a 2.6% actuary forecast prices at 2.6%, not 3.2%. L = $25,000, N = 50, T = 14 gives w = 1.0, so the same 3.2% forecast prices at 3.2% and the premium rises or the risk is declined.

Given that depth, a binding rule that ignores liquidity is theatre. Foreguild's rule is explicit:

  • Binding is earned, not assumed. Below $5,000 of two-sided liquidity or 20 unique traders, advisory. Up to $50,000 of financial impact at $5,000 and 20 traders. Above that, advisory unless the market holds $25,000, has 40 traders, and the board ratifies the decision.
  • Decision bond: the proposer posts 2% of the maximum financial impact (minimum $500). If the decision is later shown to reduce RAC90 by more than the market forecast, the bond is slashed to the reserve. This makes a proposal a bet, not a press release.
  • Insider ban: operators, auditors, market makers and their affiliates disclose positions and may not trade markets that set their own compensation or reserve. Violations void the round.
  • Red team: $10,000 a year (budgeted in the §08 legal/compliance/audit line) to pay an independent team to try to manipulate the markets; the results are published and the thresholds are adjusted. A governance system that never tests its own manipulation cost is a belief system.

Critics have warned for years that thin markets, risk premia and manipulation distort decision prices, and that a decision rule can itself bias the market it reads.161819 Foreguild does not pretend to solve those problems in general. It narrows the decision to a measured number, seeds both branches, caps the binding stake and publishes the manipulation cost. Where the market is too thin, it says so and does not bind.

The manipulation arithmetic. With $5,000 of two-sided liquidity, moving a 5% TWAP costs roughly $250–$500 in spread and adverse selection, depending on the AMM range. That is cheap relative to a $50,000 decision, which is why the binding cap is $50,000 and why a decision bond exists. At $25,000 of liquidity, the same move costs roughly $1,250–$2,500 — still cheap relative to a $250,000 decision, which is why above $50,000 the market is advisory and the board must publish a reasoned override. The rule is not “markets always decide.” It is “markets decide only where the cost of being wrong is priced.”

Who can propose, and how often

A Guild Pass is a non-transferable credential earned by 90 days of verified work, 10 settled tasks, or $5,000 of buyer spend. One active proposal per pass per quarter; a bond of 0.5% of the proposed spend (minimum $100, maximum $2,500). Agents can hold a pass through their operator wallet; the operator is liable for the bond. The monthly cycle is: proposal window (7 days), market trading (7 days), TWAP + timelock (48 hours), execution (7 days), post-mortem (30 days). Quarterly, the performance markets resolve and the board publishes the RAC90 bridge. Annually, the charter is reviewed; the five constitutional rules are not open to a simple market.

The human and legal wrapper

Foreguild Labs LLC (to be formed in Delaware) will be the operating company and the contracting party. A foundation (to be formed) will hold the brand and the verification library; the LLC will license them. The operating agreement will bind the LLC to implement market outcomes inside the five constitutional rules, subject to the board's fiduciary duty. If the board overrides a market for legality, solvency or a broken oracle, it must publish the reason within 48 hours and submit a ratification market within 7 days. That is the honest version of “futarchy inside a legal wrapper”: the market is binding where it can be, and the board is accountable where it cannot.

Not insurance. The guarantee is a contractual performance remedy sold to businesses, not an insurance policy sold to consumers. It is initially funded from the company's own reserve, not from public premiums. Before selling to anyone outside the approved business list, Foreguild obtains a legal opinion on the guarantee structure in its operating jurisdictions, and if it later cedes risk to a carrier, it does so through a licensed partner. The plan does not assume a license it does not have.

Why this drives revenue, not just legitimacy

The market's forecast is an input to the quote. If the LR90 market says the loss ratio for “Base lending protocol, high-severity fix” is 38%, the actuary prices at 38% with a credibility weight; if it says 62%, the premium rises or the risk is declined. A better forecast means a higher win rate on good risks and a lower loss ratio on bad ones. The market is not a debating society; it is the pricing engine. That is the test of whether futarchy belongs in this business: remove it, and the quote gets worse.

What would change our mind

The thesis is wrong if…

  • After 20 quotes, fewer than 20% of buyers accept a 6% guarantee premium. Then the buyer wants labour, not risk transfer; pivot to the Verify API.
  • The loss ratio stays above 60% after 100 claims. Then the class is uninsurable at viable prices; exit or reprice to a niche.
  • The average contract value stays below $2,000. Then the fixed verification cost eats the margin; the guarantee cannot scale.
  • Seer depth stays below $5,000 after 12 months of seeding. Then futarchy is advisory only; use a human underwriting committee with market input.

The thesis is stronger if…

  • Audit firms refer 5+ contracts in the first quarter without a discount. That is a distribution moat, not a favour.
  • The first 20 claims come in below 40% loss ratio. Then the premium can fall and the win rate rises.
  • Buyers renew the guarantee without renegotiating. Then the product is a subscription, not a one-off.
  • A Seer class market attracts 20+ traders and beats the actuary's forecast. Then the market is a real pricing input, and the governance story is earned.

Limitations of this plan

No Foreguild revenue, users or partners exist. The loss ratio, verification costs and error rates are targets or assumptions, not measurements. The Seer depth measurement is real but small: 127 funded conditional markets and $13,600 of liquidity is not a deep governance system. The legal characterization of the guarantee is unresolved and jurisdiction-specific. The model is sensitive to the loss ratio and the average ticket; a 10-point loss-ratio miss is $44k of Year-3 EBITDA, and a 30-point miss is $133k. The external guarantee line — $4.0M of Year-3 guaranteed value — is the most aggressive assumption; the zero-case cuts Year-3 EBITDA by about $104k, and the first 10 external guarantees are a day-90 test. The plan is written to make those weaknesses visible, not to hide them behind a total addressable market.

E. Claim census method

Input: the POIDH #1531 record, saved as data/poidh1531.json on 11 November 2026, containing 73 claims with title and description. Method: case-insensitive regular expressions over the concatenation of title and description, one pattern per theme. Counts: verification verif 42; insurance/assurance insur|assur 14; reserve ratio reserve ratio 2; loss ratio loss ratio 0; combined ratio combined ratio 0; actuarial actuar 0; cohort cohort 1; CAC/LTV \bcac\b|\bltv\b 2; TAM \btam\b|addressable 0. Limitations: summaries are shorter than full plans (median 1,028 characters); a full PDF may contain a term the summary omits. The census is a statement about the public claim text, not a judgment of the underlying work.
